  - type: "section"
    kicker: "the story"
    title: "The problem, and what is needed"
    body:
      - "Almost everything worth knowing about a population is about individuals. How long they live, whether they return, how far they move, which ones survive a bad year. Counts alone cannot answer any of it."
      - "The established way to find out is to tag animals — a fin clip, a PIT tag, an external marker — which means catching them, handling them, and accepting both the welfare cost and the effect handling has on the behaviour you then measure."
      - "It is also expensive, which caps sample sizes. A study might tag a few hundred fish and follow them, when the question really concerns tens of thousands."
      - "Meanwhile many species carry natural markings that are individually unique and stable over a lifetime. Trout spot patterns are the clearest example: no two are the same, and an individual’s pattern does not change as it grows."
      - "What is needed is a way to read those markings reliably from ordinary survey imagery, across years, at a scale that makes tagging unnecessary for most questions."

  - type: "section"
    kicker: "method"
    title: "What we do, and what it changes"
    body:
      - "We train models to encode the marking pattern of an individual into a representation that survives changes in angle, lighting and water clarity, and then to match new sightings against a catalogue of known individuals."
      - "The problem is open-set, which is the difficult part. A new image is not necessarily one of the animals already catalogued — it may be an individual never seen before, and a system that always returns its best guess will happily invent a life history."
      - "So the model reports similarity rather than identity, and every proposed match above threshold is reviewed before it enters the record. The catalogue is treated as evidence, not as output."
      - "Where this works, ordinary survey imagery becomes a mark-recapture study that nobody had to catch anything for. Survival, growth and movement fall out of imagery collected for other reasons."
      - "It also changes what is affordable. Following thousands of individuals costs roughly what following hundreds used to, which makes questions about rare outcomes — the fish that survives the drought year — answerable for the first time."

  - type: "section"
    kicker: "in hindsight"
    title: "What we got wrong first"
    body:
      - "We expected the matching model to be the hard problem and image quality to be a detail. It was the other way round. Given a clean, well-posed image the match is close to trivial; given a typical field image it is close to impossible."
      - "Most of the engineering since has gone into the unglamorous end — pose normalisation, deciding which frames are usable at all, and being willing to discard the majority of captured imagery."
      - "We underestimated the cost of a false match. A wrong identification does not simply add noise; it fabricates a specific and plausible event, a fish that moved between reaches or survived a winter it did not survive. Those errors propagate straight into survival estimates."
      - "That is why the threshold sits where it does, and why the review step has never been automated away. Erring toward no match loses data. Erring toward a match invents it."
      - "The remaining open question is how far this generalises. Spot patterns are unusually cooperative. Whether the same approach holds for species with subtler or slower-changing markings is genuinely unsettled."
