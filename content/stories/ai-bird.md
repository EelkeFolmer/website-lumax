  - type: "section"
    kicker: "the story"
    title: "The problem, and what is needed"
    body:
      - "Seabirds concentrate. A species that ranges across an ocean for most of the year will put a substantial fraction of its breeding population onto a handful of islands and cliffs for a few months. What happens on those hectares is, in practice, what happens to the population."
      - "That concentration makes colonies both the best place to measure a population and the worst place to walk. A census done on foot means someone moving through dense nesting ground at the most sensitive point in the breeding cycle, flushing adults off eggs and opening the way for gulls."
      - "The conventional alternatives all trade one problem for another. Boat-based counts see only the seaward edge. Photographs from a fixed point on one clear day give a single number with no sense of how it varies. Nobody has enough good-weather days."
      - "Avian influenza raised the stakes considerably. Outbreaks can move through a dense colony in a matter of days, and the first visible sign is dead birds on the ground — by which point the question is no longer whether to respond but how much was lost."
      - "So the information need has two halves. Nest counts accurate and repeatable enough to build a population trend, and a mortality signal fast enough that a response can still change the outcome."
      - "Both have to be delivered without adding disturbance, which rules out most of the ways such things have historically been measured."

  - type: "section"
    kicker: "method"
    title: "What we do, and what it changes"
    body:
      - "We fly the colony on a planned grid at an altitude chosen per species, high enough that the birds ignore the aircraft and low enough that the imagery resolves an individual nest. Constant ground sampling distance matters more than absolute height, so the flight plan follows the terrain."
      - "The imagery is stitched into a single orthomosaic of the colony, and detection models find and count apparently occupied nests across it. The same models flag birds that appear to be dead, which is the signal that matters during an outbreak."
      - "Every model is calibrated against ground counts on a sample of plots. Where the imagery is ambiguous the frame is sent to a person rather than guessed at, and those adjudications become the next round of training data."
      - "The platform is cloud-based because the people who need the answer are rarely the people who flew the survey. A reserve manager should be able to open last week’s count without processing a terabyte of imagery themselves."
      - "What changes is mostly frequency. A colony that was counted once a season can be counted every few weeks, by whoever is already on site, with results comparable across years because the method does not depend on who was holding the clicker."
      - "And during an outbreak the interval that matters shrinks from a fortnight to a few days, which is the difference between documenting a die-off and intervening in one."

  - type: "section"
    kicker: "in hindsight"
    title: "What we got wrong first"
    body:
      - "We assumed nest detection would be the hard part. It was not. The hard part was geometry — stitching hundreds of overlapping frames of a textureless cliff into a mosaic that does not duplicate or lose birds at the seams."
      - "Disturbance thresholds turned out to be highly species-specific, and not in the direction intuition suggests. Some species tolerate an aircraft at surprisingly low altitude; others react to a drone they cannot possibly have seen. Every survey plan now starts from the literature for that species and a cautious trial."
      - "Dead-bird detection carries a weight ordinary counting does not. A false positive can trigger an expensive and disruptive response; a false negative wastes the days that matter most. We run that classifier deliberately conservative and treat its output as a prompt to look, not a conclusion."
      - "We also underestimated how much of the value is in consistency rather than accuracy. A count that is slightly biased but biased the same way every year tells you more about a trend than a perfect count taken once."
