    title: "The problem, and what is needed"
    body:
      - "Many seabirds spend most of the year at sea and come ashore only to breed. For a few months a large part of a population gathers on a few islands, sandbanks and salt marshes, often in dense colonies. Counting those colonies is how the size and trend of a breeding population is known."
      - "Colonies are often counted on foot. A field team searches sample plots for nests several times a season, marks each nest and notes whether it holds eggs or chicks. The density in the plots is then scaled up to the whole colony. It is careful work, and a lot of it. It puts people in the nesting ground during the breeding season, the only time a breeding census can be done, and the birds are disturbed as the team walks through. The total also depends on how well a few plots represent the whole colony."
      - "AI-BIRD has its roots in West Africa. With BirdLife, we monitored West African crested tern colonies by drone in the Saloum delta in Senegal in 2015 and 2018, and in Mauritania and Guinea-Bissau in 2019."
      - "With Sovon we have since developed it on gull and tern colonies in the Netherlands and Germany, within the Wij & Wadvogels programme, funded by Vogelbescherming [ASK EELKE: confirm programme name and funder wording]."
      - "The behaviour model also recognises dead birds, for avian influenza. An outbreak can move through a dense colony in a matter of days, and the first visible sign is dead birds on the ground, by which point the question is how much has been lost.   - type: "section"
    kicker: "method"
    title: "What we do, and what it changes"
    body:
      - "We fly the colony in parallel lines with an off-the-shelf drone. Altitude is a trade-off. Flying lower gives finer detail but more flight lines, a longer flight and a drone closer to the birds. Over the gull colony on Griend, in the Dutch Wadden Sea, we flew at 28 to 37 m. That gave 8 to 12 mm per pixel, enough to recognise the species and behaviour of each large gull."
      - "The thousands of overlapping photos of each flight are stitched into one orthomosaic of the whole colony, in which each patch of ground appears once, so each bird is counted once. A detection model finds every bird and nest on it. Classification models then give each bird a species, a behaviour (breeding, sitting, flying, swimming or dead) and an age."
      - "Where we can, we fly twice in quick succession. A bird that is only sitting may move between flights, while a breeding bird stays on its nest. A bird in the same place in both flights counts as a nest, the same unit a field team counts."
      - "We check the drone counts against field counts. On Griend in 2026, at the request of Natuurmonumenten, we flew the colony twice in the breeding season and compared the result with the field team’s nest search in four sample plots of about a hectare each. On 26 May the field team found 240 active large-gull nests in the plots. For 194 of them there was a gull at the same place in both drone flights. For 47 of those 194 the match relied on a correction made by eye: a missed nest, a missed gull or a wrong species. Checking and correcting detections is part of the method, and the corrections become training data for the next count. The detector had been trained partly on labelled images from these plots, so this is a favourable test. The second flight and the 8 May flights are independent of it."
      - "The platform, built with Earth Toolsmaker, runs in the cloud. Site managers upload the photos and get back a map of the colony with every detection on it, which they can check and correct in the browser."
      - "What changes is coverage. One flight covers the whole colony, so the total no longer depends on a few sample plots, and every bird is assigned a species. The images are kept, so any count can be checked or repeated later."
    marginPull: "Fly high enough that the birds ignore the drone, low enough that the model can see."

  - type: "section"
    kicker: "in hindsight"
    title: "What we got wrong first"
    body:
      - "A bird is not a nest. A field team counts nests and follows them through the season, and the drone sees birds. On Griend, by 26 May many nests had hatched, and large-gull chicks leave the nest scrape within a day or a few days [ASK EELKE: literature source; the Griend report asks Jan Veen for one]. The parent then stands nearby rather than sitting on the nest. Of the nests with eggs, 86% matched a gull in both flights. Of the nests with chicks, 58% did."
      - "On the earlier flight, on 8 May, the opposite happened. Laying was still under way, and the drone found breeding gulls at places where the field team found a nest only later. Often both partners stood at the nest. One survey date catches part of the colony too early and part of it too late."
      - "A sample of plots is not the colony. In the drone count of 26 May the four Griend plots held breeding large gulls at 2.1 times the average density over the colony, so a total scaled up from the plots would come out high. [ASK EELKE: are Natuurmonumenten and Jan Veen happy for us to publish this before the field extrapolation is in the report?]"
      - "A model only knows the species it was trained on, and gives any other bird the nearest species it does know. Ours does not yet know great black-backed gull, common gull, oystercatcher or redshank, which matters wherever they breed alongside the gulls and terns."
