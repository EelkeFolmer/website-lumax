  - type: "section"
    kicker: "the story"
    title: "The problem, and what is needed"
    body:
      - "For a few months each year, a large part of a seabird population gathers to breed on a few islands, sandbanks and salt marshes. Counting those colonies shows how large the breeding population is and how it changes."
      - "Breeding sites are often hard to reach because islands, sandbanks and salt marshes may be cut off by open water or the tide. Counting a colony on foot means walking through the nesting ground in the breeding season, the only time a breeding census can be done, and every visit disturbs the birds."
      - "Avian influenza has made counting colonies more urgent. In 2022 the virus killed large numbers of Sandwich terns, and in 2024 black-headed gulls. An outbreak can move through a dense colony in a matter of days, and the first visible sign is dead birds on the ground, by which point the question is how much has been lost."
      - "So the need has two halves: a count of breeding birds for the whole colony, repeatable from year to year, and a way to find dead birds across a colony while a response can still help. Both are best collected from the air: without disturbance, quickly, and accurately enough to compare one year with the next."
    marginPull: "A colony is the best place to measure a population, and the air is the best place to count it from."
    marginNote: "The species model recognises eleven species, from black-headed gull and Arctic tern to avocet, eider and spoonbill."

  - type: "section"
    kicker: "method"
    title: "What we do, and what it changes"
    body:
      - "Drones are now often used to monitor breeding seabirds. We pioneered this in West Africa: with BirdLife, we monitored colonies of West African crested terns, Caspian terns, slender-billed gulls and grey-headed gulls by drone in the Saloum delta in Senegal in 2015 and 2018, and in Mauritania and Guinea-Bissau in 2019. We then started developing machine-learning models to detect birds in the drone images. On the West African colonies our first model found and classified most of 21,000 birds in 4.5 hours, against about three weeks by hand."
      - "From there we built AI-BIRD with Sovon, working on gull and tern colonies in the Netherlands and Germany. AI-BIRD turns the drone photos of a colony into a count of every bird in it. The work has financial support from the Dutch Ministry of Agriculture, Fisheries, Food Security and Nature (LVVN) and the EU’s NextGenerationEU fund, and part of it runs within the Wij & Wadvogels programme, funded by Vogelbescherming."
      - "We fly the colony in parallel lines with an off-the-shelf drone. The thousands of overlapping photos of each flight are stitched into one orthomosaic of the whole colony, in which each patch of ground appears once, so each bird is counted once. A detection model finds every bird and nest on it. Classification models then give each bird a species, a behaviour (breeding, sitting, flying, swimming or dead) and an age. The behaviour model includes the recognition of dead birds, so the same survey shows where birds have died during an outbreak."
      - "Where we can, we fly twice in quick succession. A bird that is only sitting may move between flights, while a breeding bird stays on its nest. A bird in the same place in both flights counts as a nest, the same unit a field team counts. Timing matters too. Early in the season pairs are still laying and both partners often stand at the nest; once the eggs hatch, large-gull chicks leave the nest within days and the parent no longer sits on it."
      - "An ecologist then checks the detections on the map and corrects them where needed. Each correction becomes training data, so the models learn from every survey we fly. A model only knows the species it was trained on; adding great black-backed gull, common gull, oystercatcher and redshank is next."
      - "What changes is the cost to the birds and the time to a result. Nobody walks into the colony, and one flight covers all of it, so the total no longer depends on a few sample plots. The platform, built with Earth Toolsmaker, runs in the cloud: site managers upload the photos and get the counts back soon after the flight, on a map with every detection. Every bird gets a species, and the images are kept, so any count can be checked or repeated later."
    marginPull: "21,000 birds in 4.5 hours, against about three weeks of counting by hand."
    marginNote: "On Dutch colony surveys the detector found 88% of the birds present, and 98% of its detections were correct. Species, age and behaviour were right 95% of the time on average, over 10 species and 1,398 checked detections."
