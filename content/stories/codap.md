  - type: "section"
    kicker: "the story"
    title: "The problem, and what is needed"
    body:
      - "Saltmarsh and mudflat sit between the sea and everything behind it. They dissipate wave energy before it reaches a dyke, and they do it more cheaply and more gracefully than concrete does. Coastal defence strategy across northern Europe increasingly assumes they will still be there."
      - "Whether they will depends on a race. Sediment accretes on the flats and the marsh builds upward; sea level rises underneath it. If accretion keeps pace the system persists. If it does not, the marsh drowns, and the protection goes with it."
      - "The race is decided in centimetres per year, across systems tens of kilometres wide, in terrain that is under water half the time and too soft to walk on for much of the rest."
      - "Traditional levelling gives excellent numbers at a handful of points. Satellite altimetry covers the whole system at a resolution that cannot see a centimetre. Neither answers the actual question, which is where in this system is it building and where is it losing."
      - "What is needed is elevation across the whole intertidal area, precise enough to resolve seasonal change, repeated often enough that a trend can be separated from a storm."
      - "And it has to be collected in the few hours around low water when the flats are exposed, which constrains everything about how the work is planned."

  - type: "section"
    kicker: "method"
    title: "What we do, and what it changes"
    body:
      - "We fly the exposed flats with RPAS on a grid designed for high overlap, and reconstruct the surface using Structure-from-Motion photogrammetry. The output is a dense elevation model of the whole survey area rather than a set of measured points."
      - "Ground control is what makes it absolute. Surveyed markers placed before the flight anchor the reconstruction to a national height datum, so this season’s surface can be differenced against last season’s and the result means something."
      - "Flights are planned around the tide, not the calendar. The usable window is a few hours either side of low water, and it has to coincide with light, wind and visibility that photogrammetry can work with. In practice the schedule is written by the almanac."
      - "Differencing successive surveys gives a map of change: where sediment has accumulated, where creeks have migrated, where the marsh edge has retreated. That map is the deliverable, not the elevation model itself."
      - "For coastal managers it turns an abstract question about sea-level rise into a specific one about this stretch of this system. Nature-based defence schemes can be judged on whether the marsh actually grew, rather than on whether the design said it would."

  - type: "section"
    kicker: "in hindsight"
    title: "What we got wrong first"
    body:
      - "Water is the enemy of photogrammetry, and intertidal flats are covered in it even at low tide. Standing water in runnels and pools reconstructs as noise or as a mirror, and early surveys produced beautiful elevation models of puddles."
      - "We now plan for the drainage state as carefully as for the tide, and mask standing water explicitly rather than letting the reconstruction guess."
      - "Vegetation caused the second round of trouble. Photogrammetry measures the top of what it can see, which in a vegetated marsh is the canopy, not the ground. A summer survey and a winter survey of the same unchanged marsh can differ by more than a year of accretion."
      - "The fix is partly seasonal timing and partly correction, but the honest answer is that vegetated marsh surfaces carry more uncertainty than bare flats, and we say so in the reporting rather than smoothing it away."
      - "The third lesson was about expectations. Clients often want one number for the whole system. The whole point of the method is that there is no such number — the system builds in some places and loses in others, and that spatial pattern is the finding."
