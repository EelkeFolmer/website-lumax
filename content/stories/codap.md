blurb: "Along the Wadden Sea coast, tides and storms carry mud and sand in and out, and salt marshes grow where the water is calm enough for silt to settle and plants to establish. People have lived here for more than two thousand years and built the dikes that protect the land, but those dikes also leave the salt-marshes little room to move as the sea rises. Whether the mudflats and salt-marshes keep up with the sea can only be seen by measuring it again and again. With repeated drone surveys we map, to the centimetre, where the mudflats and marshes are building up, where they are eroding and how the habitats on it change, and we make the results available in the online portal CODAP."
standfirst: "The Wadden Sea coast is shaped by the interplay of the sea, the sediment, the vegetation of the salt marshes, and people. For more than two thousand years people have lived, fished and farmed here, and they shaped the landscape by building mounds (terpen and wierden) and dikes. Ever since the last ice age, the natural dynamics have allowed the coast to rise in pace with the sea. The dikes have protected the land from floods, but they have also confined that growth to the narrow strip on the seaward side of the dike. We measure, to the centimetre and survey after survey, where salt marshes and mudflats are building up and where they are being lost, so that this knowledge can help protect the natural dynamics of the coast and be used to shape the seascapes of the future."

  - type: "section"
    kicker: "the story"
    title: "A coast that keeps moving"
    body:
      - "The Wadden coast is highly dynamic. With the rhythm of the tides and in response to storms, mud and sand are transported in, laid down and taken away again. Channels shift, flats rise and fall, and where the water is calm enough for silt to settle and vegetation to establish, salt marshes grow. The Wadden Sea is the largest unbroken system of intertidal sand and mud flats in the world, a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 2009."
      - "Salt marshes establish on high mudflats where the hydrodynamic forces are low. Glasswort and common cordgrass come first. At high tide they slow the water, so silt settles between their stems. As the surface rises the marsh floods less often and the vegetation changes, from sea aster, sea lavender and common saltmarsh grass in the low marsh to sea couch, red fescue and sea wormwood higher up. The marshes are breeding grounds for redshank and oystercatcher, high-tide roosts for waders that feed on the flats, and grazing land for geese. On the intertidal flats in front, seagrass beds, mussel beds and mats of macro-algae come and go."
      - "People have been part of this system since about 600 BC, first living on dwelling mounds that they built to protect themselves and their livestock against the floods, and from around the 11th century behind dikes. Most of the mainland salt marshes in Friesland and Groningen were made with human help. For centuries, and more systematically since the 1930s, brushwood dams were built to create sedimentation fields, and marshes grew in their shelter. For a long time the aim was to fix the coast in place. But behind a fixed dike a marsh cannot move inland as the sea level rises; it can only build upward, and where it cannot keep up, its edge erodes."
      - "A different future is possible. Where there is room, natural processes can shape the coast again. A wider marsh in front of the dike dampens the waves before they reach it. Where a dike is moved inland or opened, sediment brought in by the tide raises the land behind it as the sea rises. A coast that is allowed to move keeps pace with the sea more easily and holds more habitats than one that is fixed. People can adapt to such a coast, and live, fish, farm and work with a seascape that keeps changing."
      - "To get there, we need to understand the morphodynamics of the coast: how fast the ground is rising or falling, what habitats it holds, and how the system responds to sea-level rise. That means measuring and analysing the morphology and the habitats of whole sites regularly, and to the centimetre."

  - type: "section"
    kicker: "method"
    title: "From drone flight to map of change"
    body:
      - "Since 2019 Lumax and the University of Twente have monitored coastal morphology with drones at several locations on the mainland coast of the Wadden Sea, and in British Columbia, Canada. We started with RTK quadcopters on smaller areas, and moved to large-area mapping with the Marlyn, a fixed-wing VTOL drone from Atmos, for which we were early beta testers. One Marlyn flight at about 100 metres covers some 200 hectares of marsh and mudflat at about 13 mm per pixel."
      - "Flights are planned around low tide, preferably at spring tide, because the flats have to be exposed and drained. Every drone position is corrected with RTK or PPK GNSS, and ground check points measured in the field are used to check the accuracy of each map. Structure-from-Motion photogrammetry turns the photos into an orthomosaic and an elevation model. The maps are accurate to about 15 mm horizontally on average and to 6\u201320 mm vertically."
      - "Differencing two elevation models gives a map of change which shows where sediment has accumulated, where erosion took place, where creeks have moved and where the marsh edge has retreated. A deep learning model trained on labels classifies every pixel of each orthomosaic as mudflat, salt marsh, shells, macro-algae and other cover classes. The labelling scheme already distinguishes plant communities, so the model can learn them as more surveys are labelled. The same survey then also shows what grows where, and a series of surveys shows how habitats change over time."
      - "Wierum, on the Frisian coast, is one example. We have surveyed it since 2022. The salt marsh was shrinking and its edge had eroded into a cliff. In summer 2024 Rijkswaterstaat built rows of brushwood dams on the flat in front of it. The surveys show that the dams work. In the first year 15\u201335 cm of sediment settled inside them, while outside them the flat hardly changed. By October 2025 the gain was locally more than 50 cm. Storm Benjamin that month caused no significant change in bed level. Since then sedimentation has continued although at a lower rate."
      - "CODAP is an online portal that we built with Earth Toolsmaker to visualise the results and make the data available. It puts every survey of a site on one timeline in a web browser. Anyone can compare two dates side by side, lay the land-cover map over the orthomosaic, or draw a transect to see how the ground has changed along it. Every map can be traced back to the photos, labels and code that made it, so any two surveys are processed the same way and can be compared fairly."
    marginPull: "At Wierum, 15 mm horizontally and 6\u201320 mm vertically, across some 200 hectares in a single flight."

  - type: "section"
    kicker: "next"
    title: "What comes next"
    body:
      - "The land-cover model learns from surveys that ecologists have labelled. It is most reliable for the surfaces it has seen most often and in most seasons, and least reliable for classes that are rare or seen at only one time of year. Its accuracy is measured on areas held back from training and against observations in the field."
      - "We are developing the model in two directions. The first is more classes. The labelling scheme distinguishes zones, subzones and cover types, from bare flat and pioneer zone to high marsh, down to individual plant communities. A model that can tell these classes apart turns an orthomosaic into a map of habitats. Comparing such maps over time shows how habitats change: where pioneer vegetation advances, where low marsh becomes high marsh, and how shell banks and algal mats shift from one season to the next. Each class needs labelled examples from several seasons, because vegetation looks different in winter and in summer."
      - "The second is a model that works across sites. Vegetation types and sediment colour differ from one site to another, so a model trained on one site cannot simply be applied to the next. Every new site adds labelled surveys that make the model more general, and testing it on sites it has not seen shows how well it transfers."
    marginPull: "From an orthomosaic to a map of habitats."
