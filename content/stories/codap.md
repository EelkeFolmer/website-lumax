  - type: "section"
    kicker: "the story"
    title: "The problem, and what is needed"
    body:
      - "Along the mainland coast of the Wadden Sea, salt marshes form a narrow green band between the dike and the tidal flats. The Wadden Sea is the largest unbroken system of intertidal sand and mud flats in the world, and a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 2009. Its marshes are breeding grounds for redshank and oystercatcher, high-tide roosts for waders that feed on the flats, and grazing land for geese in spring and autumn. On the flats in front, shell banks, mussel beds and mats of macro-algae come and go."
      - "A salt marsh grows from the sea up. On the bare flat, diatoms bind the mud. Glasswort and common cordgrass come first: at high tide they slow the water, so silt settles between their stems. As the ground rises the marsh floods less often and the vegetation changes, from sea aster, sea lavender and common saltmarsh grass in the low marsh to sea couch, red fescue and sea wormwood higher up. Height above mean high tide decides what grows where."
      - "It is also a cultural landscape. People have lived on these marshes for more than two thousand years, first on dwelling mounds (terpen) raised above the floods, later behind dikes. Most mainland marshes in Friesland and Groningen were made with human help: for centuries, and systematically since the 1930s, brushwood dams laid out sedimentation fields in front of the dike, and marsh grew in their shelter. The marshes are still grazed, and the dams and the patterns they left are part of the landscape. Where the works fell into decay, the marsh edge began to erode."
      - "With the dike behind it, a mainland marsh cannot move inland as the sea rises. It has to build upward and hold its edge. Upward growth is measured in millimetres to centimetres per year; at an eroding edge the marsh can lose metres in a single storm season. Field transects and yearly aerial photographs miss most of this."
      - "To protect the natural and cultural values of the coast, managers deciding whether to restore, protect or let go need to see a whole site, often, and to the centimetre: the height of the ground, what grows on it, and how both change from one survey to the next."
    marginPull: "With the dike behind it, a marsh cannot move inland. It has to build upward and hold its edge."

  - type: "section"
    kicker: "method"
    title: "What we do, and what it changes"
    body:
      - "Since 2019 Lumax and the University of Twente have monitored coastal morphology with drones on the Frisian mainland coast of the Wadden Sea, and in British Columbia, Canada. [ASK EELKE: name the 2019 sites? The dronecoast data list Holwerd, Paesens and Deikum.] We started with RTK quadcopters on smaller areas, and moved to large-area mapping with the Marlyn, a fixed-wing VTOL drone from Atmos, for which we were early beta testers. One Marlyn flight at about 100 metres covers some 200 hectares of marsh and flat at 13–16 mm per pixel. Detail flights with a quadcopter at lower altitude reach 4–5 mm."
      - "Flights are planned around the tide: the flats have to be exposed and drained, and the light good enough for photogrammetry. Every drone position is corrected with RTK or PPK GNSS, and ground check points measured in the field test each map. Structure-from-Motion photogrammetry turns the photos into an orthomosaic and an elevation model in the Dutch national grid and height datum (RD New and NAP). In the Wierum reports the maps are accurate to 15 mm horizontally on average and to 6–20 mm vertically."
      - "Differencing two elevation models gives a map of change: where sediment has accumulated, where creeks have moved, where the marsh edge has retreated. A model trained on labels drawn by ecologists classifies every pixel of each orthomosaic as mudflat, salt marsh, shells, macro-algae and other cover classes, so the same survey also shows what grows where."
      - "Wierum, on the Frisian coast, is one example. We have surveyed it since 2023. Its marsh was shrinking and its edge had eroded into a cliff, and in summer 2024 Rijkswaterstaat built rows of brushwood dams on the flat in front of it. The surveys show that the dams work: in the first year 15–35 cm of sediment settled inside them, while outside them the flat hardly changed. By October 2025 the gain was locally more than 50 cm, and storm Benjamin that month caused no significant change in bed level. Since then sedimentation has continued at a lower rate, while the marsh cliff outside the dams is still retreating."
      - "CODAP, built with Earth Toolsmaker, puts every survey of a site on one timeline in a web browser. Anyone can compare two dates side by side, lay the land-cover map over the photo, or draw a transect to see how the ground has changed along it, without GIS software. Every map can be traced back to the photos, labels and code that made it, so any two surveys are processed the same way and can be compared fairly."
    marginPull: "15 mm horizontally and 6–20 mm vertically, across some 200 hectares in a single flight."

  - type: "section"
    kicker: "in hindsight"
    title: "What we learned along the way"
    body:
      - "Water is the main source of error. Standing water in runnels and pools, or a creek that has not yet drained, does not reconstruct as ground, and at one Wierum survey the northernmost part of the site could not be mapped accurately for that reason. We now plan for the drainage state as carefully as for the tide, and report the affected parts of a map rather than smoothing over them."
      - "Comparing surveys over years needs a fixed reference. Small systematic offsets between surveys can look like sedimentation or erosion. We use a control transect on the asphalt of the dike, whose height should not change, to measure and remove that bias, and add natural check points on stable ground next to the ones we place."
      - "A land-cover model is only as general as its training data. On test areas held back from training, the Wierum model agrees with the expert labels on 99.3% of pixels, but those test areas come from the same surveys as the training data. Mudflat, dike and road are reliable; salt marsh and shells are promising; macro-algae has been seen in one survey only. Each new labelled survey, from more seasons and more sites, makes the maps more dependable."
    marginPull: "A fixed reference on the dike separates real change from the difference between two surveys."
