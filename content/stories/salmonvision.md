  - type: "section"
    kicker: "the story"
    title: "The problem, and what is needed"
    body:
      - "Pacific salmon carry the productivity of the open ocean back into freshwater every year, and a great deal depends on how many of them make it home. Fisheries are opened and closed on those numbers. Habitat restoration is judged by them. Bears, eagles and the forest itself are fed by them."
      - "The trouble is that counting them is hard. A weir gives good numbers but blocks the river and costs a season of labour to run. Foot surveys are cheaper but depend on water clarity, weather, and the patience of the person doing the walking. Neither scales past a handful of rivers, and there are thousands."
      - "So most systems are counted rarely, or estimated from a nearby river that was counted, or not counted at all. Decisions get made anyway, on numbers with error bars nobody likes to print."
      - "What managers actually need is narrow and specific: how many fish, of which species, past which point, and when. Not a modelled estimate for the region — a count for this river, this year, delivered while the season is still running."
      - "There is a second problem underneath the first, which is that the rivers people choose to count are not a random sample. They are the accessible ones, the ones near a road, the ones with a field station already standing. What gets measured is partly a map of where it was convenient to stand."
      - "Over decades that bias hardens into received knowledge. A region looks stable because the three rivers anyone ever counted are stable, while the fifty nobody counted quietly emptied out. The gaps in the record are not neutral."
      - "Any method that claims to fix this has to be cheap enough to deploy where nobody is watching, and boring enough to run for years without a specialist babysitting it. That constraint shaped everything that follows."
    marginPull: "Most rivers are not counted. The ones that are, are counted once."

  - type: "section"
    kicker: "method"
    title: "What we do, and what it changes"
    body:
      - "We put cameras and sonar where the fish have to pass, and leave them there. Underwater video works when the water is clear enough to see through; sonar keeps counting when it is not. Between them, a station can hold a season without anyone standing in the river."
      - "The footage goes to detection models trained to find a fish, follow it across frames, and decide which way it was heading and what species it was. Every model is checked against footage counted by hand before its output is used for anything at all."
      - "Aerial survey fills in the rest of the river — where the fish are spawning, which channels are open this year, how the gravel has moved since spring. The counts and the maps are built to be read together, not filed separately."
      - "A cheaper count is not really the point. The point is that a cheap count can be repeated — on more rivers, more often, for longer — and repetition is what turns a number into a trend somebody can act on."
      - "Where this is running, managers watch a run develop instead of reconstructing it afterwards. Restoration gets judged on the fish that came back rather than the metres of gravel laid."
      - "None of it removes the need for people in the field. Somebody still has to choose where a station goes, and notice when a model has quietly started counting driftwood. The work moves from tallying to judgement, which is where it belonged."

  - type: "section"
    kicker: "in hindsight"
    title: "What we got wrong first"
    body:
      - "The first season was mostly a lesson in how much of ecological fieldwork is logistics. Batteries in cold water. A lens that fogged at dawn every day for a week. A mount that held perfectly until the first serious rain moved the gravel it was bolted into."
      - "The models had their own version of the same problem. A detector trained on clear-water footage found fish beautifully until the river turned, and then found nothing at all without ever indicating it had stopped working."
      - "We also learned that species identification is where the difficulty actually lives. Counting silhouettes is close to solved. Telling two similar species apart in three seconds of murky footage, reliably enough that a management decision can rest on it, is a genuinely open problem."
      - "So the current setup is deliberately conservative. Where the model is unsure it says so and the frame goes to a person. That keeps the count honest and, as a side effect, produces exactly the training data the next version needs."
      - "It is slower than the version in the funding application. It is also the only version anybody should be willing to publish numbers from."
